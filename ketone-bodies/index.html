Ketone Bodies

“Ketone bodies” is the name for three related compounds the liver produces from fat when glucose is in short supply: beta-hydroxybutyrate (BHB), acetoacetate (AcAc), and acetone. They are a normal fuel, not a disease. Their presence in blood is what the term ketonemia names — and each of the three is read by a different measurement layer.

Governing idea

There is no single “ketone.” There are three ketone bodies, related but distinct — and which one you are measuring depends on how you measure.

What Ketone Bodies Are

When carbohydrate availability is low — during fasting, prolonged exercise, or a very low-carbohydrate diet — the liver increases fatty-acid oxidation and converts the products into ketone bodies through a process called ketogenesis. These compounds travel in the blood and serve as an alternative fuel for tissues including the brain, heart, and skeletal muscle, which can oxidize them for energy.

Ketone bodies are therefore a normal part of energy metabolism, not inherently a sign of illness. What their level means depends on the context in which they appear — the distinction this whole reference is built around. See the Signal Map for how context changes interpretation.

The Three Ketone Bodies

Ketone body Role Where it is read
Beta-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) The most abundant ketone body in blood at elevated states, and a stable energy substrate. Chemically it is a hydroxy acid rather than a true ketone, but it is conventionally grouped with the ketone bodies. Blood (meters and labs)
Acetoacetate (AcAc) The primary product of ketogenesis and the compound BHB interconverts with. It is chemically less stable and can break down to acetone. Urine strips (nitroprusside)
Acetone A minor ketone body formed largely by spontaneous decarboxylation of acetoacetate. It is volatile, so some is exhaled — the source of “ketone breath.” Breath analyzers (sensor)

This is the key to the whole measurement picture: blood testing emphasizes BHB, urine strips detect AcAc, and breath devices estimate acetone. They are three windows onto the same metabolism — not three readings of one number. See laboratory context for how the methods compare.

How the Three Relate

BHB and acetoacetate interconvert, and the balance between them tracks the metabolic (redox) state: when metabolism is strongly shifted toward fat use, more of the pool sits as BHB, which is why BHB predominates in blood at elevated ketone states and why it is the compound clinical monitoring emphasizes. Acetone forms as a one-way by-product when acetoacetate loses carbon dioxide, and because it is volatile it leaves partly through the lungs. See beta-hydroxybutyrate for the biochemistry of the dominant blood marker.

A Note on Naming

The term “ketone bodies” is a biochemical convention rather than a strict chemical category. Acetoacetate and acetone contain a ketone group; beta-hydroxybutyrate does not, yet it is counted as a ketone body because it is part of the same metabolic pool. This is why precise language matters on this site: “ketones,” “ketone bodies,” “ketosis,” and “ketonemia” are related but not interchangeable. The student guide lays out the full terminology ladder.

What “Ketone Bodies Present” Does Not Mean

The presence of ketone bodies, on its own, does not:

  • Indicate a disease — ketone bodies are a normal fuel produced during fasting, exercise, and low-carbohydrate intake
  • Equate to ketoacidosis, which is a clinical syndrome defined by more than the presence of ketones
  • Carry the same meaning across people and contexts — the same level can be expected in one situation and worth clinical attention in another
  • Substitute for clinical assessment when symptoms or risk factors are present
